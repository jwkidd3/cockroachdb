📡 Section 1: Change Data Capture in Depth

Getting data out, correctly

About 10 minutes, five slides. Two flavours (core to the SQL session, enterprise to a sink), what the envelope options cost, the resolved-timestamp frontier as the consumer's correctness tool, at-least-once meaning duplicates are normal, and what a schema change does to a running feed. The frontier slide is the one students have not seen elsewhere — spend the time there.

Students saw a core changefeed in Lab 3 Part H if they did the optional part; Lab 13 starts from there and goes to Kafka.

Two Flavours

Core

EXPERIMENTAL CHANGEFEED
  FOR shop.orders;
  • Streams to the SQL session
  • No license, no sink
  • Dies with the session
  • Debugging only

Enterprise

CREATE CHANGEFEED
  FOR TABLE shop.orders
  INTO 'kafka://...'
  WITH updated, resolved='10s';
  • Runs as a resumable job
  • At-least-once delivery
  • Survives restarts and rebalances
  • Everything in production

Sinks: Kafka, webhook, cloud storage (S3/GCS/Azure), Google Pub/Sub.

Core changefeeds stream to the SQL client that created them — a single consumer, gone when the session ends; free. Enterprise changefeeds are jobs that write to a sink (Kafka, cloud storage, webhook), survive restarts, and can be paused and resumed; licensed. Both need kv.rangefeed.enabled.

Lab 13 does both. The core one has a capture gotcha worth naming: redirect it to a file and you get nothing unless you use --format=csv and line-buffered output, because the default table format buffers the whole stream to size columns.

Terms: rebalanc — the allocator moving replicas or leases to even out capacity or load, or to repair replication after a failure. changefeed (core) — a change stream delivered to the creating SQL session; one consumer, ends with the session, no licence. changefeed (enterprise) — a long-running job publishing row changes to a sink; survives restarts, pausable; needs a licence. sink — a changefeed's destination (kafka://, cloud storage, webhook); dialled by the database node, so it must be reachable from inside the cluster network. resolved timestamp — a changefeed watermark meaning every change at or before this timestamp has been emitted. at-least-once — every change is delivered, some more than once; consumers must be idempotent. enterprise licence — unlocks incremental/revision_history backups, enterprise changefeeds and multi-region SQL; free for training; ships in .license.env; bound to an empty organization name.

The Resolved-Timestamp Frontier

This is the part everyone gets wrong. Changefeed messages arrive out of order across ranges. A row you receive at 14:05 may have a timestamp of 14:02, and a 14:01 row may still be in flight.

A resolved message is the cluster promising: "no further message will arrive with a timestamp below this value."

QuestionAnswer
Is my view current as of 14:00?Yes, iff frontier ≥ 14:00
Can I close yesterday's books?Yes, iff frontier ≥ midnight
Did I miss events during the outage?No, iff you restart from the last frontier

A resolved message says: every change up to this timestamp has been emitted; nothing older is still coming. A consumer that buffers rows and applies them only when the frontier passes them gets ordering across an at-least-once stream.

Lab 13's consumer.py does exactly that and prints each frontier advance. One operational note from the test: a resolved watermark every few seconds means the Kafka console consumer never goes idle, so its --timeout-ms (an inactivity timeout) never fires — students stop it with Ctrl+C.

Terms: range — a contiguous slice of the sorted key space (~512 MiB) and the unit of replication, rebalancing, splitting and merging. resolved timestamp — a changefeed watermark meaning every change at or before this timestamp has been emitted. frontier — in a consumer, the highest resolved timestamp seen; rows at or below it are final and can be applied in order.

At-Least-Once Means Duplicates Are Normal

After a job restart, a lease transfer, or a rebalance, messages are re-emitted. Your consumer must be idempotent — three ways:

TechniqueHowBest for
Upsert by PKINSERT … ON CONFLICT DO UPDATEMaterialized copies
Timestamp guardApply only if updated > last_seenOut-of-order safety
Dedup ledgerStore (key, ts) seen-set with TTLSide effects

For side effects — emails, charges, notifications — the ledger is the only safe option. You cannot "re-send" an email idempotently by upserting a row.

Restarts, retries, and rebalances all replay messages. The consumer must be idempotent: keep a ledger of key to last-applied timestamp and skip anything at or below it. That ledger is what makes replays harmless.

Lab 13 restarts the changefeed with a cursor and watches the consumer skip the duplicates. The design point for students: idempotency is the consumer's job, not something the producer can promise.

Terms: leaseholder — the one replica per range that serves all reads from its local copy and proposes all writes; a new leaseholder must apply every committed write before serving. rebalanc — the allocator moving replicas or leases to even out capacity or load, or to repair replication after a failure. at-least-once — every change is delivered, some more than once; consumers must be idempotent. idempotency key — a client-supplied identifier stored with an operation's result so a retry returns the stored result instead of repeating the work. TTL — row-level expiry: ttl_expire_after on a table, swept by a background job on a cron.

Acronyms: PK — primary key · TTL — time to live — row-level expiry

The contract rule: additive changes (a new nullable column) are safe. Renames and type changes are not. Version your consumer's schema handling the same way you version an API.

The hidden cost of a paused feed: its protected timestamp stops MVCC garbage collection. A changefeed left paused for days grows your storage and slows every scan. Check SHOW CHANGEFEED JOBS for the feed nobody remembered.

Additive changes — a new nullable column — flow through; the envelope simply grows and a consumer that ignores unknown fields keeps working. Renames and type changes are breaking. schema_change_policy decides whether the feed stops, backfills, or ignores.

Lab 13 adds a column under a live feed and watches the new field appear. Tie back to Day 4's CI/CD slide: the same additive-only rule applies to online schema changes.

🔌 Section 2: Application Integration Patterns

Retries, outbox, idempotency, pooling

About 12 minutes, seven slides, then Lab 14. The chain: 40001 is a protocol, so write the retry loop once and correctly; a retried transaction with a side effect needs the outbox; an outbox consumer delivers at least once, so the receiver needs an idempotency key; then choose optimistic vs pessimistic per hot spot; then size the pool.

Every slide here has a Part in Lab 14 except pooling, which is the optional Part E.

SQLSTATE 40001 Is Not an Error

Under SERIALIZABLE, a transaction that would violate serializability is aborted and must be retried by the client. That is the concurrency-control protocol, not a failure.

for attempt in range(1, MAX_ATTEMPTS + 1):
    try:
        with conn:                       # commit on success, rollback on error
            with conn.cursor() as cur:
                body(cur)                # the ENTIRE transaction, re-executed
        break
    except psycopg2.errors.SerializationFailure:
        if attempt == MAX_ATTEMPTS: raise
        time.sleep(random.uniform(0, min(0.5, 0.005 * 2**attempt)))  # jitter!

Two mistakes to avoid: retrying only the failing statement (the values you read are stale), and backoff without jitter (all your clients collide again at exactly the same moment).

Restate Day 2's point for application developers: 40001 is the database refusing to commit a wrong answer under SERIALIZABLE. The application re-runs the transaction. Every driver has a helper; use it.

Lab 14 runs the same Python with and without a retry loop under 40 concurrent threads. Without it, callers fail; with it, they succeed, and some give up at a sane retry cap under extreme contention — which the lab treats as correct behaviour, not a bug.

Terms: range — a contiguous slice of the sorted key space (~512 MiB) and the unit of replication, rebalancing, splitting and merging. SERIALIZABLE — the only isolation level; transactions behave as if run one at a time, and anomalies are prevented by aborting with 40001. 40001 / retryable error — SQLSTATE 40001: the transaction could not commit serializably and must be re-run from BEGIN; not a failure.

Before You Write a Retry Loop

❌ Read-modify-write

BEGIN;
SELECT balance FROM accounts
  WHERE id = $1;
-- app computes bal + 1
UPDATE accounts SET balance = $2
  WHERE id = $1;
COMMIT;

✅ One statement

UPDATE accounts
SET balance = balance + 1
WHERE id = $1;

Atomic server-side. No retry loop needed. No round trip in the middle of the transaction.

Use the driver's helper rather than hand-rolling: crdbpgx.ExecuteTx (Go), run_transaction() (SQLAlchemy).

Reduce the contention first: shorter transactions, no user think-time inside BEGIN, the sharded counter, per-tenant co-location, FOR UPDATE on genuinely hot rows. A retry loop is the last line, not the first.

Lab 14 measures optimistic versus FOR UPDATE on the same hot rows: about 120 ops/s with 175 retries versus 160 with none. The loop is still required — FOR UPDATE reduces retries, it does not eliminate them.

Terms: 40001 / retryable error — SQLSTATE 40001: the transaction could not commit serializably and must be re-run from BEGIN; not a failure.

Terms on this slide: run_transaction — the retry helper in the CockroachDB driver adapters — re-runs the whole transaction on 40001

The Outbox Pattern

The dual-write problem: your service writes an order to the database and publishes an event to Kafka. If the process dies between them, the two systems disagree forever. No amount of retry logic fixes this.

BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO orders (account, amount) VALUES ($1, $2) RETURNING id;
  INSERT INTO events_outbox (topic, aggregate, payload)
    VALUES ('order.created', $order_id, $payload);
COMMIT;
-- CDC ships events_outbox downstream. One transaction, one truth.

The dual-write problem: the application writes the database and then Kafka, and one of them fails. Outbox fixes it by writing the business row and an event row in one transaction, then letting a changefeed publish the outbox table. Atomicity comes from the database; delivery comes from CDC.

Lab 14 builds it and proves the atomicity by making a transaction fail and showing neither row exists. Lab 13's changefeed is the publisher.

Terms: 40001 / retryable error — SQLSTATE 40001: the transaction could not commit serializably and must be re-run from BEGIN; not a failure. outbox — writing the business row and an event row in one transaction, then publishing the event table with a changefeed; replaces the dual write. dual write — the application writing the database and then a message broker separately, where one can fail without the other.

Designing the Outbox Table

CREATE TABLE events_outbox (
  id        UUID NOT NULL DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  created   TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  topic     STRING NOT NULL,
  aggregate UUID NOT NULL,
  payload   JSONB NOT NULL,
  PRIMARY KEY (id)
) WITH (ttl_expire_after = '7 days', ttl_job_cron = '@hourly');
DecisionWhy
UUID primary keyAppend-only at high rate — a sequential key hotspots the rightmost range
Row-level TTLThe outbox is a buffer, not a log. Without TTL it grows forever
No status column, no UPDATEA polled WHERE status='pending' queue serializes on one range

The anti-pattern it replaces: SELECT … WHERE status='pending' FOR UPDATE LIMIT 100 then UPDATE … SET status='sent'. Every worker contends on the same rows; throughput caps out no matter how many workers you add.

UUID key so inserts spread; a TTL so published events expire without a cleanup job; the payload as JSONB. Never delete from it in the application path — that is a second write and contention on the rows the changefeed is reading.

Point out the TTL is a table option, ttl_expire_after, swept by a background job on a cron. In Lab 3 students saw that the schedule exists immediately but the job only appears once it has fired.

UUID — a random 128-bit key (gen_random_uuid()) that spreads inserts uniformly across ranges; no time order. outbox — writing the business row and an event row in one transaction, then publishing the event table with a changefeed; replaces the dual write. Idempotency Keys

CREATE TABLE idempotency (
  key      STRING PRIMARY KEY,   -- client-supplied, one per user action
  account  UUID NOT NULL,
  response JSONB NOT NULL,       -- what we returned the first time
  created  TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
) WITH (ttl_expire_after = '24 hours');

Claim the key in the same transaction as the work. Claim it in a separate transaction and a crash between the two leaves the key claimed and the work undone — every retry then returns "already processed" for work that never happened.

A concurrent duplicate loses the race on the primary key and rolls back its whole transaction, including the order. Exactly one order exists.

The client supplies a key; the server does the work only if the key is unseen and returns the stored result otherwise. Under retries every caller gets the same answer. The insert of the key and the business write must be one transaction.

Lab 14 runs ten concurrent callers with the same key: all ten get the same order_id, nine are replays. This needs the retry loop too — the test found that without it, contention on the key row aborted all ten.

Terms: 40001 / retryable error — SQLSTATE 40001: the transaction could not commit serializably and must be re-run from BEGIN; not a failure. UUID — a random 128-bit key (gen_random_uuid()) that spreads inserts uniformly across ranges; no time order. idempotency key — a client-supplied identifier stored with an operation's result so a retry returns the stored result instead of repeating the work. TTL — row-level expiry: ttl_expire_after on a table, swept by a background job on a cron.

Optimistic vs Pessimistic Concurrency

OCC (version column)

UPDATE accounts
SET balance = $1, version = version + 1
WHERE id = $2 AND version = $3;
-- 0 rows => someone won; re-read

No locks. Best at low contention and when there's think-time between read and write.

SELECT FOR UPDATE

BEGIN;
SELECT balance FROM accounts
  WHERE id = $1 FOR UPDATE;
UPDATE accounts SET balance = $2
  WHERE id = $1;
COMMIT;

Writers queue instead of racing. Best at high contention on a known row.

Lab 14 benchmarks both. Measure your actual contention before choosing — the "obvious" answer flips depending on it.

Optimistic: read, compute, write, and let 40001 catch a conflict at commit. Pessimistic: SELECT FOR UPDATE at the start so conflicts wait instead of restart. Optimistic wins when conflicts are rare; pessimistic wins when a few rows are hot.

Lab 14 measures both on the same rows. One thing the lab surfaced that is worth telling students: a psycopg2 connection left in a transaction holds a stale snapshot, so verification reads need a fresh autocommit connection.

Terms: contention — transactions touching the same rows at overlapping times; surfaces as waits or retryable errors. SELECT FOR UPDATE — takes the row lock at read time so conflicts wait instead of restarting; the lock is in leaseholder memory in v23.2.

Acronyms: OCC — optimistic concurrency control

Connection Pooling

SettingRule
Per app instance(cores × 2) + spindles, validated against the measured knee
Cluster totalUnder ~4× total vCPU across all nodes
PgBouncer modetransaction pooling (session pooling wastes connections)
TimeoutsSet statement_timeout and pool acquisition timeouts

An oversized pool adds no throughput. It moves the queue from your app into the database, where it's harder to see and more expensive to drain. Little's Law: concurrency = throughput × latency. 2,000 QPS at 8 ms is 16 connections, not 200.

Transaction pooling breaks session-scoped features — use SET LOCAL, not SET.

Each connection is a session on a gateway node; pools should be sized from the concurrency knee measured in Lab 8, not from the number of application threads. Too many connections means queuing inside the database instead of in the pool, where you cannot see it.

Spread the pool across nodes — a load balancer or a multi-host connection string — or one gateway does all the routing work.

Terms: concurrency knee — the last concurrency level at which throughput still grows; past it latency rises for nothing. connection pool — a set of reused client connections; size it from the measured concurrency knee, not from thread count.

The rolling-deploy rule: a migration must be compatible with both the old and the new application version, because both run at once. Add-then-backfill-then-switch-then-drop, across three deploys — never rename in one step.

Tooling: Flyway, Liquibase, Atlas, golang-migrate, Alembic — all work. Take a backup before every migration deploy; it costs minutes and buys a rollback.

Additive migrations run online and are safe to deploy without a window: add column, add index, add constraint NOT VALID then validate. Renames, drops, and type changes need the expand-migrate-contract sequence over several deploys.

Migration tools work unchanged — Flyway, Liquibase, Alembic — with one habit: never wrap a schema change and data change in the same transaction. Lab 8 ran CREATE INDEX under load; that is the online behaviour this slide relies on.

Terms: online schema change — DDL that runs as a background job while the table serves traffic; additive changes are safe, renames and type changes are not. backup: full / incremental / revision_history — full captures every row at one time; incremental captures changes since the last; revision_history keeps every version for point-in-time restore (licensed).

Phase 3  Dual write, PG authoritative               ← rollback: stop CRDB writes
Phase 4  Shadow reads, compare, serve PG            ← rollback: stop shadow reads
Phase 5  Flip: CRDB authoritative, PG still written ← rollback: flip back (minutes)
Phase 6  Stop writing PG, decommission              ← rollback: restore from backup

The flip gate — evidence, not a date

  • Replication lag < 1 s sustained for 24 h
  • Shadow-read divergence = 0 over 24 h
  • Every query at or under its PostgreSQL latency
  • A tested rollback with a measured RTO
  • Backups running on the target, restore drill completed

Bulk load, then replicate changes until in sync, then flip, and be able to flip back. MOLT Fetch does the load and replication; MOLT Verify does the row-by-row comparison. In this course both run as containers — cockroachdb/molt on the cluster network, so the URLs use crdb1 and the PostgreSQL container's name.

The cutover step everyone skips is naming the rollback before flipping. Lab 15 has students write it down.

Terms: decommission — online removal of a node: replicas are moved off while it keeps serving; refused when there is nowhere to move them. dual write — the application writing the database and then a message broker separately, where one can fail without the other. backup: full / incremental / revision_history — full captures every row at one time; incremental captures changes since the last; revision_history keeps every version for point-in-time restore (licensed). RPO / RTO — RPO: how much data may be lost (time since the last backup left the box). RTO: how long until service returns. Both measured, not declared.

Acronyms: CRDB — CockroachDB · PG — PostgreSQL

When NOT to Migrate

✅ CockroachDB earns it

  • Survivability requirements (zone/region)
  • Write throughput beyond one machine
  • Data locality / residency rules
  • Multi-region low-latency reads with strong consistency
  • No tolerance for failover windows

❌ Stay on PostgreSQL

  • Single region, one instance is fine
  • Heavy analytical scans and window functions
  • Deep extension dependence (PostGIS specifics)
  • Small team, no distributed-systems experience
  • The pain you have isn't one of the ✅ list

Being able to argue the ❌ case credibly is what makes the ✅ case believable.

Single region, fits one node, never needs to survive a zone loss, heavy use of Postgres extensions or stored procedures: stay on Postgres. Say this plainly. The credibility it buys is worth more than a marginal migration.

The decision question from Day 1: what has to keep working when a node, a zone, or a region disappears? If the honest answer is "it can be down for an hour", the migration may not pay for itself.

Terms: locality — the --locality startup flag (region=…,zone=…) the allocator uses to spread replicas across failure domains.

The Order of Operations

Work every page in this order. Skipping ahead is how an hour goes to the wrong hypothesis.

QuestionWhere to look
1Is the cluster healthy?Console Overview: live nodes, unavailable / under-replicated ranges, CPU per node
2Is it one statement?SQL Activity → Statements by latency; Insights
3Is it one range?Hot Ranges; SHOW RANGES … WITH DETAILS
4Is something waiting?Transactions (contention); transaction_contention_events; Jobs
5Is something growing?Storage; range size vs live data; kv_protected_ts_records

Healthy cluster + uneven CPU → the work is uneven (step 3). Healthy cluster + low CPU + high latency → requests are waiting, not working (step 4).

The order is by cost of being wrong. Step 1 rules out the outage class in thirty seconds — if a range is unavailable nothing else matters. Step 2 asks whether the pain has a name; Statements sorted by latency or execution count usually gives it one. Step 3 asks whether the pain has an address — one range on one leaseholder is the hot-range signature from Day 1 and Lab 8. Step 4 is for the case where CPU is low and latency is high: nobody is working, everybody is queued — contention, or a job. Step 5 is the slow-burn class: storage, GC, protected timestamps.

The two rules in the box are the ones that save the most time. Uneven CPU on a healthy cluster is a placement or key problem, never a hardware one. Low CPU with high latency is a wait, and the database can tell you who is waiting on whom.

Terms: Hot Ranges — the Console page (and /_status/v2/hotranges) listing ranges by QPS, writes and CPU with their leaseholder; there is no SQL view for it. contention — transactions touching the same rows at overlapping times; surfaces as waits or retryable errors. protected timestamp — a record (owned by a backup, changefeed or import job) that stops garbage collection of MVCC versions newer than it; visible in crdb_internal.kv_protected_ts_records.

Acronyms: CPU — central processing unit · SQL — Structured Query Language

The Two Mistakes That Cause Outages

1. No --locality

Kubernetes knows the pods are in three zones. CockroachDB does not, unless you tell it. Without --locality, the allocator can put all three replicas of a range in one zone — and you find out when that zone fails.

2. Scaling down before decommissioning

# WRONG: removes the pod, replicas go offline instantly
kubectl patch crdbcluster crdb -p '{"spec":{"nodes":3}}'

# RIGHT: move replicas off while the node still serves, THEN remove it
kubectl exec cockroachdb-client-secure -- ./cockroach node decommission 5 \
  --wait=all --certs-dir=/cockroach/cockroach-certs --host=crdb-public
kubectl patch crdbcluster crdb -p '{"spec":{"nodes":4}}'

Decommission is slower because it is correct.

One: no locality, so the scheduler puts all three replicas of a range on nodes in the same zone. Two: scaling the StatefulSet down before decommissioning, which deletes a pod and its replicas instantly instead of draining them.

Decommission first, then scale. The same rule appeared in Lab 1 (three nodes cannot shrink to two) and Lab 16 Part E restarts nodes one at a time for the same reason. Slower because it is correct.

Terms: range — a contiguous slice of the sorted key space (~512 MiB) and the unit of replication, rebalancing, splitting and merging. replica — one copy of a range on one store; three per range by default. allocator — the loop on every node that adds, removes and moves replicas and leases to satisfy zone configs, liveness, capacity, load and diversity. decommission — online removal of a node: replicas are moved off while it keeps serving; refused when there is nowhere to move them. locality — the --locality startup flag (region=…,zone=…) the allocator uses to spread replicas across failure domains.
